The point of it

What a Ryoko Pro is actually for

It replaces two bad habits: paying your carrier to use its network abroad, and connecting to whatever open network happens to be nearby.

A phone taken overseas does one of two things. It roams, which the Federal Communications Commission warns about in plain terms — rates may be much higher because of additional roaming fees on foreign mobile networks, and for most US customers domestic plans do not cover usage abroad at all. Or it hunts for free WiFi, which means hotel lobbies, airport terminals and coffee shops, each with its own login page and its own queue.

Ryoko Pro sidesteps both. It carries a SIM of its own, connects automatically to the closest tower wherever it is switched on, and then broadcasts a private network that only your devices join. The FCC's own travel advice is to use Wi-Fi spots because WiFi does not count towards a mobile data allowance — this is that idea with the hotspot in your own pocket instead of somebody else's lobby.

The money

Ryoko Pro against a roaming bill

Roaming charges are the thing most travellers are actually trying to avoid, and they have an unpleasant habit of arriving late.

The FCC makes two points worth carrying with you. The first is that phones and apps download data on their own while the handset is simply switched on, so charges accumulate without anybody deliberately browsing. The second is about timing: roaming charges may not appear on your bill for an additional billing cycle after your return — which is why the shock usually lands weeks after the holiday photographs.

Ryoko Pro's answer is to take your phone off the foreign cellular network entirely. The handset goes into airplane mode with WiFi on, joins the Ryoko network, and the only data relationship that exists is between the pocket router and its own SIM. The seller states there are no roaming fees worldwide on that connection.

One device, one connection, and a carrier bill that does not change shape.

Security, honestly

Ryoko Pro and the public WiFi question

A private hotspot genuinely removes one category of risk. It is worth being precise about which one, because this is a subject marketing pages routinely overstate.

Public networks carry a real caution from the US government. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency puts it as: although these Wi-Fi hotspots can be convenient, they are not always secure, potentially exposing you to online risks. The National Security Agency's guidance for people working in public settings makes the same point, warning that they could be putting their personal and organizational data, credentials, and devices at higher risk. Carrying your own network means you are not joining an unknown one, and that is a genuine improvement.

Now the correction. The Federal Trade Commission's current position is more relaxed than the old folklore: because of the widespread use of encryption, connecting through a public Wi-Fi network is usually safe. The protection that matters is the encryption between your browser and the site — the FTC tells you to look for a lock symbol or https in the address bar — and that protection is identical whether you are on a hotel network or your own router. A hotspot changes which network you are on. It does not encrypt your traffic, and it cannot help if the site at the other end is run by a scammer, which the FTC names as the risk encryption does not solve.

If you want the extra layer, the FTC's guidance on VPN apps is that they can help shield personal information sent over a public, open Wi-Fi network, while recommending you research the app first, review its permission requests, confirm it encrypts your information, and check whether it shares that information with third parties. A router and a VPN do different jobs, and buying one is not buying the other.

What a Ryoko Pro will not do

Five things worth knowing before the box arrives, rather than after.

It does not make signal

The device finds the nearest tower; it cannot invent one. In a true dead zone a pocket router is as offline as the phone beside it, and the seller's own warranty terms name weak local coverage and provider outages as outside its control.

500MB is a starter, not a plan

The included allowance gets you connected. Ongoing use runs on top-ups, which is a cost to budget for rather than a surprise to discover in week two.

It is not a VPN

It changes which network you are on. The encryption that protects what you send is between your browser and the website, and it works the same either way.

"Up to" means up to

150 Mb/s and eight hours are ceilings. Mobile speed follows the local network, and battery life follows how hard the device is working.

You cannot open it and return it

The published return window is 60 days, but it applies to packages that are unopened and in original condition. This is the single most important line on this page: it is a change-of-mind window, not a trial period.

None of these five is a complaint about the product. They are the places where a confident sales page and the seller's own small print do not quite meet, and a buyer is better off knowing where those are.

Honest fit

Is a Ryoko Pro right for you?

It suits one kind of traveller very well and another kind barely at all.

It fits if you cross borders more than once or twice a year; if you travel with family or colleagues and would otherwise be buying data several times over; if you work from the road and cannot afford a conference network collapsing mid-call; if you spend time in RVs, campsites or rural places where fixed connections are unreliable; or if you would simply rather not hand your details to every café login page you meet.

It fits less well if you travel once a year on a carrier plan that already includes the destination; if your trips are to places with patchy cellular coverage, where no router can help; if you need a single device online for a few minutes a day, which a local SIM may cover more cheaply; or if what you actually want is encryption rather than connectivity, in which case the FTC's VPN guidance above is the more relevant reading.

The honest test is the arithmetic in the pricing section: your carrier's daily roaming rate, multiplied by the days you will genuinely be abroad, against the device and its data. If that sum is close, buy nothing for now.

Plain English

Ryoko Pro terms worth knowing

Seven expressions that appear on the listing or in the policy pages.

Mobile hotspot
A small router with its own cellular connection, which turns mobile data into a WiFi network that several devices can share. That is the category this device belongs to.
Roaming
Using your home carrier's service on somebody else's network abroad, usually at a markup. The FCC warns that for most US customers a domestic plan does not cover usage overseas at all.
Top-up
Adding data to the SIM after the included allowance runs out. It is the ongoing cost of owning the device, separate from the price of the hardware.
Mb/s
Megabits per second, the unit mobile speed is quoted in. Eight megabits make one megabyte, so a 150 Mb/s link moves roughly 18 megabytes a second at its ceiling.
Phishing domain
A web address set up to impersonate a real one so people hand over passwords or card details. The listing's own example is "playpal.com" standing in for "paypal.com".
Encryption
The scrambling that stops anybody on the same network reading what passes between your browser and a website. The FTC's shorthand for checking it is present: a lock symbol or https in the address bar.
Original condition
The state a returned package has to be in for the 60-day window to apply. On this policy it means unopened, which is why the window is best understood as covering a change of mind.
